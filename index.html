01

Orientation

Mesenteric venous thrombosis is the least common cause of acute mesenteric ischaemia, the one most likely to be survived, and the one most likely to be missed. It is also, uniquely among the mesenteric ischaemias, a haematological disease.

Three facts shape every decision in this document.

It is an outflow problem

Arterial inflow continues while venous return fails. Pressure rises, the wall becomes oedematous, then haemorrhagic. Infarction requires involvement of the venous arcades and vasa recta, producing complete venous occlusion — which is why the clot can look dramatic on CT while the bowel is still salvageable.

Medicine beats surgery

Most patients are treated successfully with therapeutic anticoagulation and supportive care alone. Heparin is the first move in every patient without a major contraindication. Endovascular therapy and laparotomy are escalation steps for those who deteriorate, not first-line options.

The cause is the diagnosis

Systemic prothrombotic factors are found in roughly 60–70% of patients. A third of cases have a local trigger. Finding an occult myeloproliferative neoplasm, cirrhosis, or malignancy changes anticoagulation duration from months to lifelong — and may be the first sign of the underlying disease.

02

Definitions & nomenclature

Four abbreviations are used almost interchangeably in the literature and mean different things. Getting them apart is the first step in reading any MVT paper — and a routine viva opener.

MVTMesenteric venous thrombosis
Thrombosis within the superior mesenteric vein, with or without extension into the portal or splenic vein. This is the common working definition. Acute mesenteric venous thrombosis may occur with obstruction of the SMV, the inferior mesenteric vein, the splenic vein or the portal vein, and involvement of more than one venous segment is frequent — most often the portal vein and SMV together, followed by the splenic vein and inferior mesenteric vein.
AMVTAcute mesenteric venous thrombosis
MVT in a patient whose symptoms began within four weeks of presentation. The four-week cut-off is explicitly arbitrary. Mean duration of symptoms at presentation is commonly reported as six to 14 days.
CMVTChronic mesenteric venous thrombosis
Symptoms lasting longer than four weeks without bowel infarction, or MVT found incidentally on abdominal imaging. Acute and chronic MVT are successive stages of the same disease with the same causes, but they require different management.
VMIVenous mesenteric ischaemia
The acute onset of symptoms in the presence of AMVT. This — not the radiological presence of clot — is the clinical entity the ESVS guideline is built around. AMVT may lead to VMI; it does not always do so.

What the guideline deliberately excludes

Chapter 7 is narrower than "splanchnic vein thrombosis". The following are named as outside its scope, and you should not apply its recommendations to them uncritically:

  • Budd–Chiari syndrome, with or without liver cirrhosis
  • Isolated portal vein obstruction
  • Isolated outflow obstruction caused by hepatic veno-occlusive (sinusoidal obstruction) disease
  • Hepatic disorders associated with congestive heart failure or chemotherapy
Why the boundary matters

Much of the evidence base underpinning MVT anticoagulation — the individual patient data meta-analysis, the international registry, the rivaroxaban cohort — is drawn from splanchnic vein thrombosis populations that include isolated portal and splenic vein thrombosis. Outcomes differ substantially by segment: in a large cohort study, patients with isolated splenic vein thrombosis had the highest ten-year recurrence-free survival at 97%, while those with isolated MVT had the lowest at 60%. When a trial is quoted at you, ask which veins it actually studied.

03

Anatomy & pathophysiology

The bowel's arterial supply is protected by an extraordinary collateral network. Its venous drainage is not. That asymmetry explains why venous occlusion behaves so differently from arterial occlusion.

The drainage anatomy

The abundant venous drainage of the viscera is via the inferior and superior mesenteric and splenic veins, culminating in the portal vein. There is one final common pathway. Contrast this with the arterial side, where the coeliac artery, SMA and IMA are connected by the pancreaticoduodenal arcades of Rio Branco and Bühler, by the arcades of Riolan and Villemin, and by the marginal artery of Drummond — a macroscopic anastomotic system in which each of the three major vessels has the ability to supply the entire viscera.

The viscera receive 10–20% of cardiac output at rest, rising to 35% in the postprandial state, starting 10–30 minutes after a meal and continuing for up to three hours.

How congestion becomes infarction

The sequence matters because it explains the clinical trap: a patient with a large clot and severe pain may have an almost unremarkable abdomen and a normal lactate.

SMV involvement carries a 33–45% risk of haemorrhagic bowel infarction if left untreated. Fever and peritoneal signs are the clinical markers of this transition. Pneumatosis intestinalis and portal venous gas are its radiological markers.

Mechanistic note for haematologists

At capillary level, the submucosal network supplies the villi and microvilli — the most metabolically active layer — and includes anastomoses at the base of the villi which allow compromised flow to be redirected away from the mucosa while continuing to perfuse the muscularis and serosa. The result is ischaemic necrosis of the mucosa with preservation of the integrity of the bowel basement membrane, which the guideline notes may in fact be life saving. This is why mucosal injury and full-thickness infarction are not the same event, and why an endoscopic or early radiological appearance can look worse than the segment's actual fate.

The prognostic headline

In-hospital mortality for AMVT has fallen to around 10%. Set this against acute arterial mesenteric ischaemia, where the ESVS guideline states the condition is associated with a 50% mortality risk, and non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia, where the mortality rate is approximately 70%. Of the three mesenteric ischaemias, MVT is by a wide margin the survivable one — provided it is recognised and anticoagulated.

Why the incidence figures disagree

The Swedish population-based figure of 2.7 per 100 000 person-years rests on a study of 402 patients with an autopsy rate of 87%; the Finnish figure is 0.5 per 100 000. A five-fold difference between two comparable Nordic health systems is a statement about ascertainment, not biology. Without post-mortem confirmation, MVT is substantially under-counted. Editorial note: the practical inference is that your local incidence is higher than your local diagnosis rate.

Risk factors & aetiology

MVT is almost never idiopathic on proper investigation. Systemic prothrombotic factors are identified in 60–70% of patients and a local trigger in 30–40% — and the two frequently coexist, which is the point most often missed.

30–40%have a local triggering factor

Most MVT can be considered as provoked, by either permanent or transient risk factors. The distinction is not academic: it is the axis on which the recommended duration of anticoagulation turns (see section 11).

The full differential of cause

Permanent (persisting) factors

  • Solid cancer — intra-abdominal malignancy in particular
  • Liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension
  • Myeloproliferative neoplasms — polycythaemia vera, essential thrombocythaemia
  • Inherited thrombophilia — prothrombin G20210A, factor V Leiden, antithrombin, protein C and protein S deficiency
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome
  • Chronic liver disease

Transient / local factors

  • Intra-abdominal inflammation or infection — pancreatitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, IBD
  • Recent surgery
  • Hormonal therapy
  • Trauma
  • Cytomegalovirus infection — rare, but may act synergistically with a G20210A mutation
  • Severe SARS-CoV-2 infection — a similar association has been described
The trap: a local trigger does not exclude a systemic cause

Appendicitis, pancreatitis or diverticulitis can each provoke MVT, and it is tempting to stop the investigation there. But systemic prothrombotic factors are present in 60–70% of patients overall, and a local trigger in only 30–40% — the two sets overlap substantially. Finding the appendix does not mean you have found the reason. Recommendation 46 (Class I, Level B) mandates investigation for intra-abdominal malignancy, inflammatory disease, myeloproliferative neoplasm, CMV and SARS-CoV-2 infection, and chronic liver disease in all patients with MVT — not only in those without an obvious local cause.

06

The haematology workup

MVT is the venous thrombosis most likely to be the presenting feature of an occult myeloproliferative neoplasm. Screening for JAK2 V617F is not a routine thrombophilia add-on — it is a diagnostic test for a different disease.

JAK2 V617F and myeloproliferative neoplasms

AMVT is a common first clinical manifestation in patients with newly diagnosed myeloproliferative disease such as polycythaemia vera or essential thrombocytosis. The JAK2 mutation is diagnostic of myeloproliferative disease and appears to be linked to the development of AMVT.

32.7%JAK2 mutation prevalence in patients with AMVT (95% CI 25.5–35.9%)Dentali 2009 meta-analysis

The clinical consequence

In that 52%, MVT was the first manifestation of the underlying condition. The guideline's inference is explicit: it is important to screen for JAK2 V617F in patients with MVT without evidence of other major systemic or local risk factors.

Editorial note for haematologists. An odds ratio of 53.9 with a confidence interval spanning 13.1 to 222.5 tells you two things at once — the association is real and very large, and the precision is poor because the underlying studies are small. Treat the point estimate as directional, not exact. The clinically actionable figure is the 32.7% prevalence: roughly one in three patients presenting with AMVT carries the mutation, and half of those will declare an MPN on follow up. A normal blood count at presentation does not exclude it; masked polycythaemia in the presence of splanchnic thrombosis and hypersplenism is a well-recognised problem, and the guideline's recommendation is to test the mutation, not to screen on the full blood count alone.

Thrombophilia testing — who, and when

Cohort studies have found a high prevalence of thrombophilia in MVT, including over-representation of factor V Leiden and the prothrombin G20210A gene mutation, or deficiencies of antithrombin, protein C and protein S, and antiphospholipid antibodies including lupus anticoagulants. A systematic review of 14 studies found a higher prevalence of prothrombin G20210A, antithrombin deficiency and protein S deficiency in MVT compared with both the general population and patients with a single venous thromboembolic event at another site.

It remains unclear to what extent these factors have implications for prognosis and duration of therapy after MVT. The guideline is candid about the resulting practice: with this high prevalence of prothrombotic factors, many experts tend to offer patients with permanent risk factors indefinite anticoagulation, despite the lack of evidence for such treatment.

Antiphospholipid syndrome

Patients with recurrent venous and/or arterial thrombosis or with recurrent foetal loss may be considered for testing for antiphospholipid syndrome, including lupus anticoagulants, anticardiolipin antibodies, β2 glycoprotein, and deficiencies of antithrombin, protein C and protein S.

Practical sequencing

Editorial note. The guideline lists the assays but does not address timing. Standard haemostasis practice applies: lupus anticoagulant testing is unreliable during acute thrombosis and on anticoagulation, particularly on a vitamin K antagonist or a direct factor Xa inhibitor; antithrombin is consumed by heparin; and protein C and S are vitamin K dependent and fall on warfarin. If antiphospholipid syndrome is being considered — and in MVT it should be, given the recurrent thrombosis phenotype — the pragmatic approach is to take anticardiolipin and anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies at any time, and defer lupus anticoagulant and the natural anticoagulant assays until a point at which the result can be interpreted. A confirmed diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome also changes anticoagulant choice, since direct oral anticoagulants are not the agents of choice in triple-positive disease.

Infection-associated MVT

Cytomegalovirus

Although rare, CMV has been found to be associated with AMVT and might act synergistically in patients with a G20210A mutation. This is specifically named in recommendation 46 as a condition to investigate for.

SARS-CoV-2

A similar association was found between severe SARS-CoV-2 infection and AMVT. Also named explicitly in recommendation 46.

No biomarker will help you

Do not order a lactate or D-dimer to rule this in or out

Like arterial mesenteric ischaemia, there are no specific and reliable biomarkers available to diagnose MVT. The guideline goes further for the arterial and non-occlusive forms and issues Class IIIb recommendations against the practice:

  • Recommendation 31 (Class IIIb): a single measurement of a biomarker such as lactate or D-dimer to confirm or rule out the diagnosis in patients with a clinical suspicion of acute mesenteric ischaemia is not recommended.
  • Recommendation 42 (Class IIIb): the same statement for non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia.

This is a change from the 2017 guideline, which recommended using D-dimer to rule out acute mesenteric ischaemia. The test that decides the diagnosis is the CT.

Non-specific pain early, localised tenderness later, fever and peritonism when it has gone wrong. The temporal pattern is more diagnostic than any single sign.

Fever and peritoneal signs are not the presentation of mesenteric venous thrombosis. They are the presentation of its complication.

Two named syndromes to recognise

Pylephlebitis

Infectious thrombophlebitis of the portal vein and its branches. Features: high fever, malaise, abdominal tenderness and sepsis. May be accompanied by liver abscesses. It is a rare complication of intra-abdominal infections caused by appendicitis, pancreatitis and diverticulitis.

Why it matters therapeutically: antibiotics have not been shown to lower mortality or shorten hospital stay in MVT generally, but pylephlebitis is one of the named indications where they are indicated — alongside bowel perforation, sepsis secondary to bacterial translocation, and septic thrombophlebitis.

Portal cavernoma

The chronic manifestation: the obstructed portal vein is replaced by a network of collateral veins. Complete occlusion of the portal vein, or of its two main branches, is virtually always associated with portal hypertension and the development of portosystemic collaterals.

The classical presentation — ruptured oesophageal or gastric varices, or biliary symptoms related to portal cholangiopathy (jaundice, cholangitis, cholecystitis, or pancreatitis) — is rare. Do not wait for it.

Diagnosis & imaging

One test makes this diagnosis: contrast-enhanced CT with an arterial and a portal venous phase. Everything else is supportive or misleading.

RECOMMENDATION 49Class ILevel CChanged
Contrast enhanced computed tomography scanning with imaging in the arterial and portal phases (and optional non-contrast CT run) is recommended for patients suspected of mesenteric vein thrombosis.
What changed in 2025Rephrased, and the level of evidence was downgraded from B to C. The recommendation itself became no weaker — it remains Class I — but the evidence behind it was reassessed as resting on lower-quality studies.
References: Salim et al. (2018); Henes et al. (2017)

The imaging protocol

  • In patients with unexplained, serious and/or longstanding abdominal complaints, a three-phase CTA with 1 mm maximum slice thickness is indicated.
  • CTA of the abdomen with intravenous contrast injection and imaging in the arterial and portal venous phase is the most important and accurate diagnostic tool and the imaging investigation of choice.
  • Such a CT is accurate enough to confirm or refute the diagnosis of arterial or venous mesenteric ischaemia.
  • The venous phase accurately visualises both the extent of thrombosis within the portomesenteric venous system and secondary abnormal intestinal findings.
Mention the suspicion in the request

A new Class I recommendation in the 2025 guideline (recommendation 32) prompts clinicians to state the suspicion of acute mesenteric ischaemia in the referral for CT angiography, because doing so is associated with better diagnosis and outcomes. A CT requested as "abdominal pain, ?cause" is not the same test as a CT requested as "?mesenteric ischaemia — arterial and portal venous phases please". The related recommendation 33 (Class I, unchanged) specifies urgent CTA with contrast enhancement in arterial and venous phases with ≤ 1 mm slices, and an optional non-contrast run, regardless of renal function.

09

Acute management

Heparin first, in everyone without a major contraindication. Then supportive care, and then watch the trajectory — because the trajectory, not the CT appearance, decides what happens next.

Goals of treatment

The goal of AMVT treatment is to prevent thrombus propagation and to promote mesenteric vein recanalisation, with the aim of preventing bowel infarction, portal hypertension, and thrombosis recurrence. In CMVT, the additional goals are the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal bleeding and portal cholangiopathy.

Most patients with AMVT are treated successfully with therapeutic anticoagulation and supportive treatment. Endovascular treatment and surgery are the next steps in deteriorating patients.

RECOMMENDATION 50Class ILevel CUnchanged
Anticoagulation with unfractionated or low molecular weight heparin as first line therapy is recommended for all patients with acute mesenteric vein thrombosis.
Reference: Wang et al. (2022)

Why heparin, and why unfractionated in particular

  • In the absence of major contraindications, systemic anticoagulation should be initiated soon after the diagnosis is made, with UFH or LMWH, to reduce the risk of thrombosis propagation, VTE recurrence, and overall mortality.
  • In the acute phase, unfractionated heparin carries the advantage that its action can be reversed by protamine if laparotomy becomes necessary or bleeding complications occur.
  • Early anticoagulation results in recanalisation in 40–80% of patients, and complete recanalisation is associated with less extensive thrombosis.
Reading the 40–80% recanalisation figure

Editorial note. The range is wide because it aggregates heterogeneous cohorts with different clot burdens and different imaging follow-up intervals. The directional statement inside it is the useful one: complete recanalisation is associated with less extensive thrombosis. Extent of clot at diagnosis is therefore both a prognostic marker and, implicitly, an argument for starting anticoagulation before the clot extends further — not for waiting to see whether the patient declares themselves.

Supportive treatment — initiate immediately

Start at once

  • Pain control
  • Fluid and electrolyte supplementation
  • Bowel rest

Add when indicated

  • Nasogastric aspiration — for ileus, abdominal distension, and intractable nausea and vomiting
  • Blood transfusion — for patients presenting with bleeding
  • Parenteral nutrition

Antibiotics — a specific, limited indication

Not routine in MVT

The use of antibiotics has not been shown to be associated with a lower mortality rate or shorter hospital stay in two small retrospective studies of MVT. Antibiotics are indicated if the patient suffers from:

  • Bowel perforation
  • Sepsis secondary to bacterial translocation
  • Pylephlebitis
  • Septic thrombophlebitis

Note the contrast with arterial disease. For acute mesenteric ischaemia generally, recommendation 41 is Class I — treatment with antibiotics is recommended (rephrased in 2025, with the level of evidence downgraded from B to C). The venous chapter does not extend that blanket recommendation to MVT. Editorial note: this is a genuine and examinable distinction, and one that local antibiotic stewardship will ask you to justify.

Endovascular therapy & surgery

Escalation is for deterioration on anticoagulation, not for extensive clot on the initial scan. This is the part of the chapter where the 2025 guideline moved — and the part where the evidence is weakest.

Endovascular treatment

RECOMMENDATION 51Class IIbLevel CNew in 2025
Endovascular venous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy may be considered for patients with acute venous mesenteric ischaemia who deteriorate during anticoagulant therapy.
Why this is newSince 2017, more evidence has accumulated on the effectiveness of intravenous thrombolysis, and this new Class IIb recommendation reflects that accumulation. Note the precise trigger: deterioration during anticoagulant therapy — not clot burden, and not failure to recanalise on interval imaging.

Who is a candidate

Patients with persisting symptoms, worsening abdominal pain after initiation of anticoagulation, or those developing signs of peritonitis may be considered for endovascular treatment, if necessary followed by diagnostic laparoscopy or exploratory laparotomy.

Read the 89% versus 93% carefully

A four-percentage-point advantage for endovascular treatment in a systematic review looks persuasive until you read the guideline's own verdict on it: "the level of evidence for the effectiveness of endovascular treatment of AMVT is low because of obvious selection bias and poor quality studies." Patients selected for endovascular treatment are not the same patients as those managed medically. Set the 93% against an 18% bleeding complication rate from the thrombolysis meta-analysis, and the Class IIb grading — may be considered — is the honest reading of the data. Editorial note: a candidate who quotes 93% without quoting the selection bias caveat will be asked about it.

Open surgery

Patients with persisting or worsening symptoms, organ failure, and those with perforation or signs of peritonitis require open surgical intervention. The aim of surgery is to remove irreversibly ischaemic bowel and preserve as much bowel as possible.

The two hard problems

1. When to operate

Choosing when to perform laparotomy. Too early and you resect salvageable bowel in a patient who would have recanalised; too late and you are operating on perforation and sepsis.

2. What to resect

Deciding when a bowel segment is irreversibly injured. The distinction between irreversible and reversible intestinal ischaemia is notably difficult, especially in AMVT.

Duration of anticoagulation

This is where the 2025 guideline changed most, and where it makes a recommendation that will surprise anyone trained on standard VTE duration rules.

Transitioning from heparin

  • After the acute phase of AMVT, anticoagulation should be maintained to prevent progression and recurrence of thrombosis, which is estimated to be 2.7% in one year.
  • Oral anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist can usually be started two to three weeks after the onset of AMVT, when the phase of acute ischaemic injury has passed.
  • Historically, VKA is the drug of choice. Off-label use of a DOAC after AMVT in small retrospective studies suggests that rivaroxaban and apixaban may be equally as effective as VKA or enoxaparin for this indication.

Extending beyond six months

The counterintuitive recommendation

Recommendations 54 and 55 recommend extended anticoagulation beyond six months for patients with transient risk factors. In most other venous thromboembolism settings, a transient provoking factor is the reason to stop at three months. ESVS 2025 takes the opposite position here because of the consequences of recurrence in this vascular bed — extensive bowel involvement and short bowel syndrome — and because of the observed recurrence rates after discontinuation. Combined with recommendation 56, the practical effect is that almost every patient with MVT is a candidate for anticoagulation beyond six months. Editorial note: this is a deliberate and clinically significant divergence from generic VTE practice, and worth stating explicitly in any exam answer.

Extended anticoagulation must be considered during shared decision making, after carefully balancing three things. The guideline sets them out explicitly:

i. Bleeding risk

Presence of varices, low platelet count, previous bleeding episodes.

ii. Risk of recurrence

Persisting risk factors and previous venous thromboembolism.

iii. Consequences of recurrence

Extensive bowel involvement and short bowel syndrome. The reason this calculus differs from ordinary VTE.

Pitfalls & open questions

Where the guideline admits it does not know, and where clinicians reliably go wrong.

Eight clinical traps

TRAP 01
Using lactate or D-dimer to exclude the diagnosis.
Why it failsNo specific or reliable biomarker exists. A single measurement to confirm or rule out acute mesenteric ischaemia is now a Class IIIb recommendation — a reversal of the 2017 guidance, which endorsed D-dimer for rule-out. The CT decides.
TRAP 02
Stopping the aetiological workup once a local cause is found.
Why it failsSystemic prothrombotic factors are present in 60–70% of patients; local triggers in only 30–40%. Recommendation 46 applies to all patients with MVT, not to the subset without an obvious local cause.
TRAP 03
Treating a normal full blood count as excluding a myeloproliferative neoplasm.
Why it failsJAK2 V617F is present in 32.7% of AMVT, and 52% of those patients declared a myeloproliferative disease only during follow up, with MVT as the first manifestation. The guideline recommends screening for the mutation in MVT without other major risk factors — a genetic test, not a count.
TRAP 04
Diagnosing on duplex ultrasound alone.
Why it failsDuplex is insufficient to evaluate the extent of portomesenteric thrombosis and the secondary intestinal abnormalities, and needs to be complemented by CT. Extent of clot is prognostic, and duplex will not give it to you.
TRAP 05
Escalating to thrombolysis on the basis of clot burden rather than deterioration.
Why it failsRecommendation 51 specifies patients who deteriorate during anticoagulant therapy. Thrombolysis carries an 18% bleeding complication rate in the pooled data, and the apparent superiority of endovascular treatment rests on studies the guideline itself describes as subject to obvious selection bias.
TRAP 06
Applying standard VTE duration logic — transient factor, so three months and stop.
Why it failsRecommendations 54 and 55 recommend extended anticoagulation beyond six months for transient risk factors. The consequences of recurrence in this bed — extensive bowel involvement, short bowel syndrome — change the calculus.
TRAP 07
Testing for thrombophilia reflexively in everyone.
Why it failsRecommendation 48 restricts testing to selected patients who will discontinue anticoagulation after three to six months. In patients with another indication for anticoagulation, there is no indication to test. Test where the answer changes the plan.
TRAP 08
Quoting splanchnic vein thrombosis data as if it were mesenteric vein thrombosis data.
Why it failsTen-year recurrence-free survival was 97% for isolated splenic vein thrombosis and 60% for isolated MVT. The segment determines the prognosis, and most of the large anticoagulation datasets are splanchnic, not mesenteric.

What the guideline says it does not know

Unresolved issues — ESVS 2025 §11.4, venous mesenteric thrombosis

"More studies are required to assess the additional value of endovascular invasive treatments when anticoagulation fails in patients with extensive thrombosis of the mesenteric and or portal venous systems."

Read alongside the chapter text, four further questions are left explicitly open:

  • Optimal duration is unknown. "The optimal duration of anticoagulant treatment after AMVT is unknown, although there may be an argument to extend treatment beyond six months."
  • The prognostic meaning of thrombophilia is unclear. "It is as yet unclear to what extent these factors have implications for prognosis and duration of therapy after MVT."
  • Indefinite anticoagulation for permanent risk factors is practice, not evidence. "Many experts tend to offer patients with permanent risk factors indefinite anticoagulation, despite the lack of evidence for such treatment."
  • Bowel viability assessment is unsolved. The distinction between irreversible and reversible intestinal ischaemia is "notably difficult, especially in AMVT", and quantitative ICG imaging — the most promising adjunct — is used in only 7% of practices.
Why the evidence is this thin, in the guideline's own words

"The current level of evidence for diagnosis and treatment of AMVT is low because only retrospective case series with a high risk of bias have been published." More broadly, the guideline notes that diseases of the mesenteric and renal vessels are uncommon, "which hampers the collection and synthesis of high quality data". Across the whole 102-recommendation document, only two recommendations are supported by Level A evidence, 12 by Level B, and 88 by Level C evidence or consensus.

Single best answer

SBA 1 · Aetiology
A 44-year-old man presents with five days of central abdominal pain and diarrhoea. CT with arterial and portal venous phases shows thrombus in the superior mesenteric vein extending into the portal vein, with bowel wall thickening but no pneumatosis. Full blood count, liver function and CRP are unremarkable. He has no history of surgery, trauma or hormonal therapy, and no intra-abdominal inflammatory process is identified. Which single test is most likely to reveal the underlying cause?
Answer: C. The ESVS 2025 guideline states it is important to screen for JAK2 V617F in patients with MVT without evidence of other major systemic or local risk factors — precisely this scenario. The mutation is present in 32.7% of patients with AMVT (95% CI 25.5–35.9%) compared with approximately 1% of patients with VTE at other sites, with an odds ratio for the association of 53.9. Critically, 52% of patients with MVT and a JAK2 mutation were diagnosed with a myeloproliferative disease during follow up, with MVT as the first manifestation — so a normal full blood count does not exclude it, which makes E premature and C the right first step. A is specifically not recommended for confirming or excluding the diagnosis (Class IIIb). B and D are components of a thrombophilia screen, which recommendation 48 restricts to selected patients who will discontinue anticoagulation at three to six months.
SBA 2 · Acute management
A 58-year-old woman with newly diagnosed acute mesenteric venous thrombosis has generalised abdominal pain but a soft abdomen, no guarding, no fever and normal observations. She has no contraindication to anticoagulation. What is the most appropriate initial management?
Answer: B. Recommendation 50 (Class I) — anticoagulation with unfractionated or low molecular weight heparin as first line therapy is recommended for all patients with acute mesenteric vein thrombosis. Supportive treatment (pain control, fluid and electrolyte supplementation, bowel rest) should be initiated immediately. UFH has the specific advantage that it can be reversed with protamine should laparotomy become necessary. A is reserved for peritonitis, perforation, organ failure or persisting/worsening symptoms — none present here. C is a Class IIb option for patients who deteriorate during anticoagulant therapy, not for initial management. D is wrong on both agent and setting: a DOAC is a Class IIb alternative for the three-to-six-month phase, and the acute phase requires heparin and inpatient supportive care. E is not an accepted strategy; antiplatelet therapy has no role.
SBA 3 · Duration
A 39-year-old man had acute mesenteric venous thrombosis six months ago, provoked by severe acute pancreatitis which has completely resolved. He has no thrombophilia, no malignancy and no liver disease. He has had no bleeding. According to ESVS 2025, which is most appropriate?
Answer: C. This is the counterintuitive recommendation. Recommendation 54 (Class IIa) states that extended anticoagulation beyond six months with a vitamin K antagonist should be considered for patients with acute mesenteric vein thrombosis and transient risk factors; recommendation 55 (Class IIb) permits a DOAC as the alternative. In most other VTE settings a resolved transient provoking factor is the reason to stop — here it is not, because of the consequences of recurrence in this vascular bed (extensive bowel involvement and short bowel syndrome) and because recurrence rates rise after discontinuation (6.6 vs 3.4 per 100 patient-years in the individual patient data meta-analysis). The decision remains one of shared decision making balancing bleeding risk, recurrence risk and the consequences of recurrence. A is the standard-VTE answer and the intended distractor.
SBA 4 · Diagnosis
Which statement about imaging in suspected mesenteric venous thrombosis is correct according to ESVS 2025?
Answer: D. Recommendation 49 (Class I) — contrast-enhanced CT with imaging in the arterial and portal phases, with an optional non-contrast run, is recommended for patients suspected of mesenteric vein thrombosis. A three-phase CTA with 1 mm maximum slice thickness is indicated, and the venous phase accurately visualises both the extent of thrombosis and the secondary intestinal findings. A is explicitly wrong: duplex alone is insufficient and needs to be complemented by CT. B is wrong: the related Class I recommendation 33 specifies urgent CTA regardless of renal function. C is wrong — the venous phase is the one that shows the thrombus, which is why the protocol is multiphase. E is wrong; DSA has been replaced by CTA as the reference standard even for non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia in the 2025 update.
SBA 5 · Evidence
A trainee tells you that endovascular treatment is superior to anticoagulation in acute MVT, citing a systematic review reporting clinical effectiveness of 93% versus 89%. What is the single most important caveat?
Answer: B. The guideline states directly that "the level of evidence for the effectiveness of endovascular treatment of AMVT is low because of obvious selection bias and poor quality studies." Patients selected for endovascular therapy differ systematically from those managed medically, so the comparison is not like for like. This is why recommendation 51 is graded Class IIb, Level C and is restricted to patients who deteriorate during anticoagulant therapy. The associated harms matter too: pooled thrombolysis data show an 18% rate of bleeding complications (95% CI 7–32%) alongside a 58% complete recanalisation rate.
SBA 6 · Thrombophilia testing
A 62-year-old woman with acute mesenteric venous thrombosis also has permanent atrial fibrillation for which she takes lifelong anticoagulation. Which approach to thrombophilia testing is most appropriate?
Answer: A. Recommendation 48 (Class IIb) restricts thrombophilia testing to selected patients with acute venous mesenteric thrombosis who will discontinue anticoagulation after three to six months. The guideline is explicit that in patients with MVT and another indication for anticoagulation, there is no indication to test for thrombophilia — the result cannot change the management. This mirrors the ASH 2023 guideline position, which offers conditional testing for those who would otherwise stop, with very low certainty of evidence. Note that this is separate from JAK2 V617F screening, which tests for an underlying myeloproliferative neoplasm rather than for thrombophilia, and which carries its own diagnostic and haematological implications regardless of anticoagulation plans.

Viva prompts

Open each to see the structure of a strong answer. These are written as an examiner would expect them to unfold — definition, mechanism, evidence, then the caveat.

"Tell me about mesenteric venous thrombosis."

Open with the definition and the mechanism. MVT is thrombosis within the superior mesenteric vein with or without extension into the portal or splenic vein. It is the least common cause of acute mesenteric ischaemia, accounting for 11.5% of cases. Unlike arterial occlusion it is an outflow problem: arterial inflow continues, venous pressure rises, the wall becomes congested and oedematous, and infarction occurs only when the venous arcades and vasa recta are involved and venous occlusion is complete.

Give the classification. Acute if symptoms are within four weeks — an arbitrary cut-off — chronic beyond that without infarction, or as an incidental finding. Venous mesenteric ischaemia is the acute onset of symptoms in the presence of acute MVT, and that is the clinical entity the guideline addresses.

Then the two things that make it distinctive. First, it is the survivable mesenteric ischaemia: in-hospital mortality is around 10%, against 50% for acute arterial and approximately 70% for non-occlusive. Second, it is a haematological disease — systemic prothrombotic factors are found in 60–70% of patients, and it may be the first manifestation of a myeloproliferative neoplasm.

Close on management in one sentence. Contrast-enhanced CT with arterial and portal venous phases makes the diagnosis; heparin is first-line in everyone without a contraindication; most patients are managed medically; and escalation to endovascular therapy or laparotomy is driven by deterioration or peritonitis, not by clot burden.

"Why would you screen for JAK2 V617F in this patient?"

State what you are testing for. Not a thrombophilia — a clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm. JAK2 V617F is diagnostic of myeloproliferative disease, and acute MVT is a common first clinical manifestation of newly diagnosed polycythaemia vera or essential thrombocythaemia.

Give the numbers. A meta-analysis reported JAK2 mutation prevalence of 32.7% in AMVT (95% CI 25.5–35.9%) against approximately 1% in VTE at other sites, with a strong association — odds ratio 53.9, 95% CI 13.1 to 222.5. I would add that the confidence interval is very wide, so I take the direction and the prevalence figure rather than the point estimate.

Give the consequence. 52% of patients with MVT and a JAK2 mutation were diagnosed with a myeloproliferative disease during follow up, with MVT being the first manifestation. So the test identifies a disease that has not yet declared itself.

Name the indication precisely. The guideline recommends screening in patients with MVT without evidence of other major systemic or local risk factors. And a normal full blood count does not exclude it — splanchnic thrombosis with portal hypertension and hypersplenism can mask the counts, so I would test the mutation rather than rely on the count.

Anticipate the follow-up question. If positive, this becomes a permanent risk factor, which under recommendation 56 supports indefinite anticoagulation, and the patient needs formal haematological assessment for diagnosis and cytoreduction decisions.

"When would you operate?"

Name the absolute indications. Perforation or signs of peritonitis require open surgical intervention, as do organ failure and persisting or worsening symptoms despite anticoagulation. In the guideline's suggested algorithm, peritonitis at presentation is the single branch point that takes a patient straight to laparotomy.

State the aim. To remove irreversibly ischaemic bowel and preserve as much bowel as possible. The resection rate in a systematic review of 599 patients was 43.9%, though it varies widely between studies.

Acknowledge the two hard problems honestly. Choosing when to perform laparotomy, and deciding when a bowel segment is irreversibly injured. The guideline states that the distinction between irreversible and reversible intestinal ischaemia is notably difficult, especially in acute MVT.

Give the mitigations. Resection without primary reconstruction with a planned second-look laparotomy is Class IIa. Quantitative indocyanine green fluorescent imaging is a new Class IIb option as an aid to viability assessment, although it is currently used in only 7% of practices.

Finish on what surgery is not. It is not the first-line treatment. Most patients with acute MVT are treated successfully with therapeutic anticoagulation and supportive treatment alone.

"How long will you anticoagulate, and why?"

Start with the floor. Three to six months with a VKA or LMWH for all patients — recommendation 52, Class I, Level B, one of only two Level B recommendations in the chapter. A DOAC may be considered as an alternative, Class IIb.

Then make the distinctive point. ESVS 2025 recommends considering extension beyond six months even for patients with transient risk factors — recommendation 54 for a VKA and 55 for a DOAC. That diverges from standard VTE practice, where a resolved provoking factor argues for stopping.

Justify it. Two reasons. The recurrence data: in the individual patient data meta-analysis of 1635 patients, recurrent VTE was 3.4 per 100 patient-years on treatment versus 6.6 after discontinuation, and 38.5% of recurrences were in the mesenteric veins. And the consequences of recurrence in this bed — extensive bowel involvement and short bowel syndrome.

Then indefinite treatment. Recommendation 56, Class I: indefinite anticoagulation for idiopathic MVT and for patients with permanent risk factors. I would note that this recommendation is graded on consensus rather than on a study.

Close with the balance. Extended anticoagulation must be a shared decision balancing bleeding risk — varices, low platelets, prior bleeding — against recurrence risk and the consequences of recurrence. Cirrhotic patients are the hardest group: in the international registry they had both the highest thrombotic event rate at 11.3 and the highest major bleeding rate at 10.0 per 100 patient-years.

"What has changed since the 2017 guideline?"

Quantify it first. In the MVT chapter, four recommendations are new — 51, 53, 54 and 55 — and one changed, recommendation 49. Six are unchanged.

Group the new ones into two themes. First, endovascular escalation: recommendation 51 is new, permitting endovascular venous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy for patients who deteriorate during anticoagulant therapy. The guideline attributes this to evidence on intravenous thrombolysis accumulating since 2017. Second, oral anticoagulation: recommendations 53 and 55 formally accommodate DOACs for the first time, and 54 and 55 introduce extended treatment beyond six months for transient risk factors.

Explain the one change. Recommendation 49 on CT was rephrased and its level of evidence downgraded from B to C — the class remained I, so the strength of the instruction did not change, only the assessment of the evidence behind it.

Add the cross-chapter change that bears on MVT. The single-biomarker recommendations were reversed. The 2017 guideline recommended D-dimer to rule out acute mesenteric ischaemia; recommendations 31 and 42 are now Class IIIb against using a single measurement of lactate or D-dimer to confirm or rule out the diagnosis.

"Why is the evidence in this chapter so weak, and does that change what you do?"

State the position. Nine of the eleven MVT recommendations are Level C. Only recommendations 46 and 52 reach Level B. None reaches Level A. Across the whole 102-recommendation guideline, only two recommendations are supported by Level A evidence and 88 by Level C or consensus.

Give the reason the guideline gives. "The current level of evidence for diagnosis and treatment of AMVT is low because only retrospective case series with a high risk of bias have been published." More broadly, diseases of the mesenteric and renal vessels are uncommon, which hampers the collection and synthesis of high quality data.

Then answer the second half of the question properly. No, it does not change the first-line action. A Class I, Level C recommendation is still a strong instruction — heparin first, CT to diagnose — and in a disease with a 33–45% risk of haemorrhagic infarction if untreated, waiting for randomised evidence is not a neutral act. What the evidence grade does change is the conversation about extended and indefinite anticoagulation, where the recommendation rests on consensus, the datasets disagree on bleeding, and the decision should genuinely be shared with the patient.

If pressed on how it should be improved. The guideline's own answer, in its unresolved issues chapter, is international scientific collaboration through high quality registries or randomised trials, with agreement on reporting standards.

One-page summary

Recognise

  • Think of it in unexplained abdominal pain out of proportion to signs, especially with cirrhosis, cancer, an MPN, recent abdominal inflammation or surgery, or a personal or family thrombotic history.
  • Pain is non-specific early; localised tenderness develops later. Fever and peritonism mean infarction, not presentation.
  • > 75% have had symptoms for two to three days before diagnosis. Mean symptom duration 6–14 days.
  • Do not use lactate or D-dimer to rule it in or out — Class IIIb.

Diagnose

  • Contrast-enhanced CT, arterial + portal venous phases, ≤ 1 mm slices, optional non-contrast run. Regardless of renal function. Class I
  • State the suspicion in the request — associated with better diagnosis and outcomes.
  • Vascular findings: thrombus in SMV ± PV ± SV ± IMV.
  • Non-vascular findings: bowel wall thickening, bowel dilatation, mesenteric fat stranding → then pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas (advanced).
  • Duplex alone is insufficient — must be complemented by CT.

Treat acutely

  • UFH or LMWH first line in everyone without a major contraindication. Class I UFH is reversible with protamine if laparotomy becomes necessary.
  • Supportive at once: pain control, fluids and electrolytes, bowel rest. Add NG aspiration for ileus/distension/vomiting, transfusion for bleeding, parenteral nutrition.
  • Antibiotics are not routine in MVT. Indicated for perforation, sepsis from bacterial translocation, pylephlebitis, septic thrombophlebitis.
  • Peritonitis at presentation → laparotomy. No peritonitis → anticoagulate and watch the trajectory.
  • Deteriorating on anticoagulation, no peritonitis → consider endovascular thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy. Class IIb
  • Deteriorating with peritonitis → laparotomy. Resect irreversibly ischaemic bowel, preserve as much as possible; consider no primary reconstruction plus second look.

Investigate the cause — in everyone

  • Class I: intra-abdominal malignancy, inflammatory disease, myeloproliferative neoplasm, CMV, SARS-CoV-2, chronic liver disease.
  • JAK2 V617F where no other major systemic or local risk factor is found — 32.7% prevalence, and MVT is the first manifestation of an MPN in half of those who are positive.
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome if recurrent MVT or recurrent foetal loss. Class IIa
  • Thrombophilia panel only in selected patients who will stop anticoagulation at three to six months. Class IIb
